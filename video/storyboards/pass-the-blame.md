# Pass the Blame

*A country under pressure. Everyone feels it, everyone points at someone else. So who, or what, is actually to blame?*

- **Story:** Germany's healthcare, seen through one ordinary operation, a groin hernia repair. Every side at the table says the problem lies elsewhere. The film follows the blame from seat to seat until it reaches what is really at fault.
- **Runtime:** about 9½ minutes, in 8 chapters. About 1,150 words of narration at about 130 words per minute, plus pauses.
- **Voice:** a curious, even-handed investigator. Each side gets a fair hearing; the narrator never takes one side's word for it.
- **Look:** a dusk-lit 3D diorama with soft data overlays (style B + a gentler D). See "Visual language".
- **Supersedes:** *One Price* (`one-price.md`) and *The Hospital That Must Change Shape*. Facts are carried over from their checked ledgers.

---

## The answer the film earns

Blame sits on three layers, revealed one at a time:

1. **Forces no one chose.** An ageing population, weaker growth, the energy shock. They squeeze everyone and are nobody's fault.
2. **A design that splits responsibility.**
   - The federal government writes the rules.
   - The 16 states plan hospitals and are meant to pay for buildings.
   - Insurers, hospitals and doctors negotiate prices, and when they can't agree, an arbitration committee decides.
   - Building money and treatment money come through separate pipes.

   No one owns a patient's whole journey, so every problem can be passed on. This is the core of the film.
3. **Specific choices that can be changed.**
   - A blended price that pays nothing extra for training or for the hard cases.
   - A readiness payment that moves existing money rather than adding any.
   - States covering only about half of what hospitals need for buildings.
   - Payment rules that for years made the overnight stay the default.

**Ending:** blame is less useful than ownership. The question is not "whose fault?" but "who will own the whole?"

> *Accuracy guardrails:*
> - The positions each side voices are summaries of their published stances, attributed as "argues". There are no invented quotes from real people.
> - Policy choices are presented as choices: deliberate, with intended aims and unintended effects.
> - Hospitals are shown as constrained, not powerless.

## Visual language: "Dusk diorama + soft data"

- **World:** Germany as a miniature model under a glass dome, in low-poly 3D like style B. Buildings for each actor:
  - **Federal ministry** (Berlin), a domed building.
  - **State capital** (Saarbrücken), a town hall.
  - **Insurers' headquarters.**
  - **Klinikum** on its wooded hill.
  - **A doctor's practice.**
  - **A patient's home.**
- **Money:** glowing pipes run between buildings. **Amber** for treatment money (insurers → hospitals and practices), **sky blue** for building money (states → hospitals). Two pipes that never join is the film's central image of split responsibility.
- **Decisions:** thin white lines dropping from Berlin and the state capital onto everything below.
- **Blame:** a soft coral spotlight beam that swings from building to building. It is the moving camera's companion in every chapter.
- **Pressure:** a gentle gauge in the sky over the dome, rising through the film. When it peaks, the dome itself creaks.
- **Data overlays:** soft, rounded frosted-glass cards floating beside the buildings, with clean Inter numerals and a gentle glow. Calm navy and teal, not black. No scanlines, glitch or terminal fonts. Numbers count up, lines draw on, small charts grow out of the buildings themselves.
- **Camera:** slow orbits and push-ins, with a tilt-shift blur at the top and bottom of the frame so it reads as a miniature. Dusk lighting deepens into night over the film.
- **Sound:** a soft ambient bed, a muted whoosh as the blame beam swings, a gentle hit on each reveal. Nothing harsh.
- **Captions:** clean subtitles, no boxes.

>
> To find out who's right, we'll follow one small operation through the whole machine — and see where the blame finally lands.

- *Insurers: "too costly"*

## Chapter 1 · The pressure
**0:45 – 2:00** · *Card: "Why is everyone under pressure?"*

**Visual:**
- The pressure gauge rises.
- Over the dome: the age grid at 20 in 100, then 25.
- A soft chart grows from the insurers' headquarters: spending line +7.1% against income line +4.1%, with the gap widening.
- A wage slip floats with a 42% slice.
- A factory on the edge of the model dims, with its gas pipe fading.
- A small growth line beside it: flat, a small rise, flat again.

**Narration:**
> Start with what nobody chose.
>
> One German in five is sixty-seven or over; by 2035, one in four. More patients, fewer people paying in. Health already costs more than five hundred billion euros a year.
>
> Contributions for health, pensions, care and unemployment take about forty-two percent of a typical salary. This year the public insurers' spending grew seven percent; their income, four. Without action, the government projected a gap of tens of billions a year by 2030 — and in July it passed a law to close it.
>
> And the economy that pays the bills has stalled: three years of almost no growth, energy-hungry industry down nearly a fifth since 2021, Chinese factories competing with German ones. Growth is expected to pick up a little, then fade again.
>
> Not a collapse. But money will be tight for a long time — and when money is tight, people look for someone to blame.

**Cards:**
- *Aged 67+: ~20% (2024) → ~25% (2035)*
- *Health spending €538bn (2024)*
- *Contributions ≈42%*
- *Insurers H1 2026: spending +7.1%, income +4.1%*
- *Gap without action: €19bn (2027) → €44bn (2030); stabilisation law, July 2026*
- *GDP −0.8 / 0.0 / +0.2% (2023–25); forecast +1.3 / +1.1 / +0.4% (2026–28)*
- *Energy-intensive output −18% vs 2021*

## Chapter 2 · One operation, one price
**2:00 – 3:05** · *Card: "What did Germany change?"*

**Visual:** Zoom down to the patient's home and the hospital on its hill. A bar race floats over Europe: England 79%, Sweden ~80%, Denmark "almost all", Germany 14%. A glowing price tag (€2,082.60) settles over two paths: the road home and a lit hospital window.

**Narration:**
> Our operation: a groin hernia repair. Germany does about a quarter of a million a year.
>
> In England and Sweden, about four in five patients go home the same day. In Germany, closer to one in seven — partly because the old payment rules made the overnight stay the default. For most patients that night isn't needed; studies of carefully selected patients find no difference in complications.
>
> So from 2024 Germany set one price for this operation and many others — the same whether the patient goes home or stays the night. For a simple repair this year: about two thousand euros.
>
> A sensible fix. So why is everyone at the table unhappy?

**Cards:**
- *Day-case share: Germany 14% (2019) vs ~80% in England and Sweden*
- *Beds per 100,000 people: Germany 759 · EU 507*
- *2026 price, simple one-sided repair: €2,082.60; complex variants up to €3,639.27*
- *Swiss study (2023): no difference for selected patients; 4% admitted after all*

## Chapter 3 · The insurers' case
**3:05 – 4:10** · *Card: "Insurers: 'It's costing us more.'"*

**Visual:** The blame beam lands on the insurers' headquarters. Their amber pipe glows brighter as money flows faster. A receipt prints from the building and rolls to +€360m. Two arrows push it: "price" and "volume".

**Narration:**
> The insurers point at the bill. Their own research institute found that across these same-price operations, spending in 2025 was about three hundred and sixty million euros higher, after inflation.
>
> The price blended the old hospital rate with the old outpatient rate — so operations once done cheaply in doctors' practices now earned more. And cases rose by seventeen percent.
>
> So is it the hospitals' and doctors' fault? Not quite. Insurers don't set these prices alone: they negotiate them with hospitals and doctors. This year the three sides couldn't agree, and an arbitration committee decided.
>
> The beam moves on.

**Cards:**
- *WIdO (the insurers' AOK research institute), Sep 2026: ~€360m more in 2025 across all same-price services; cases +17%*
- *Prices: negotiated jointly; 2026 decided by arbitration*

## Chapter 4 · The hospital's case
**4:10 – 5:30** · *Card: "Hospitals: 'We're left with the hard cases.'"*

**Visual:**
- The beam lands on the Klinikum.
- A sorting conveyor sends simple patients down the road to the practice; complex ones (frail, repeat, large, emergency) roll back up the hill.
- The sky-blue building pipe from the state capital is only half full.
- The night falls a little: the hospital's emergency band stays lit.
- A card shows readiness money as an arrow that loops back into the same amber pipe ("moved, not added").

**Narration:**
> The hospitals point at what's left behind. One price suits the simplest patients, and they're the easiest to treat elsewhere — industry voices say some outpatient centres pick exactly those. The frail, the repeats, the large hernias and the emergencies still come up the hill.
>
> And the hospital must stay ready all night. A readiness payment is coming by 2030 — but it's mostly existing money moved into a fixed budget, not new money.
>
> Then there are the buildings. Those are meant to be paid by the states. The hospitals' association says the states cover only about half of what's needed. Two in three hospitals surveyed lost money in 2024. In Saarbrücken, the city is setting aside fifteen million euros this year to keep its hospital steady.
>
> Hospitals didn't write these rules. But they're not powerless either — they still choose which patients go home, how their theatres run, who they work with.
>
> The beam moves on.

**Cards:**
- *Outpatient hernia patients are younger and healthier (registry)*
- *Readiness payment: ~60% of operating payments reallocated; full from 2030*
- *States' building money: "only just over half" of need (DKG, the hospitals' association)*
- *DKI survey: 66% lost money (2024)*
- *Saarbrücken: €15.4m city support (2026)*

## Chapter 5 · The surgeons' case
**5:30 – 6:35** · *Card: "Surgeons: 'The price ignores what surgery takes.'"*

**Visual:** The beam lands inside the hospital, on a miniature operating theatre. Two clocks float (70 and 94 minutes). Two small rings: keyhole 72% inpatient, 34% outpatient. A Denmark inset: a keyhole icon and a "home" icon side by side.

**Narration:**
> The surgeons point at what the price can't see. Most agree many patients could go home the same day. But the recommended repair — keyhole surgery with a mesh, with less pain afterwards — costs more on the day. In Germany, three quarters of inpatient repairs used it; only a third of outpatient ones. In Denmark, keyhole and same-day go together.
>
> And the next surgeon has to learn. Supervised residents in one study took ninety-four minutes; experienced surgeons, seventy — safely. The price has no line for those twenty-four minutes. The surgeons' society warned of exactly this.
>
> So — is it the government's fault?

**Cards:**
- *HerniaSurge 2023: keyhole (laparo-endoscopic) repair recommended where expertise exists*
- *Herniamed 2019: keyhole 72% inpatient vs 34% outpatient*
- *Frankfurt study 2025: 94 vs 70 minutes, no extra complications*
- *DHG (the surgeons' society), April 2024*

## Chapter 6 · Up the chain
**6:35 – 7:45** · *Card: "Berlin and the states: 'Not us.'"*

**Visual:**
- The camera rises out of the hospital and up the white decision lines to the state capital and Berlin.
- The beam swings between the two capitals, back and forth, faster and faster.
- Then the camera pulls far back and the whole machine comes into view: two pipes that never join, decision lines from two capitals, and a negotiating table with three chairs and a gavel.
- The beam slows, flickers and has nowhere to land.

**Narration:**
> Follow the decisions upward. Berlin writes the rules: the single price, the readiness payment, a fund of up to fifty billion euros to help hospitals restructure. It argues there are too many beds and too much care done too rarely to be done well.
>
> The states plan the hospitals and are meant to pay for the buildings. Several pushed back on Berlin's reform as an intrusion into their planning.
>
> Berlin points to the states. The states point to Berlin. Both point to the negotiators. The negotiators point to the law.
>
> Step back and you see why. The money for buildings and the money for treatment come through different pipes. Rules come from one capital, planning from another, prices from a table where no side can decide alone. No one owns a patient's whole journey — from the doctor's practice, to the operating theatre, to the bed at home.
>
> In a machine like this, blame never has to land anywhere.

**Cards:**
- *Federal: Hybrid-DRG law (2022), hospital reform KHVVG/KHAG, transformation fund up to €50bn to 2035*
- *States: hospital planning + investment duty; Saarland earmarked €85m for the new Saarbrücken campus, still in planning*
- *Self-administration: insurers + hospitals + doctors; arbitration when they disagree*

## Chapter 7 · Where the blame belongs
**7:45 – 9:20** · *Card: "So who is to blame?"*

**Visual:**
- Three layers lift out of the diorama like floors of a model, each labelled.
- **Layer 1, forces:** age grid, growth line, gas pipe. Grey, fixed.
- **Layer 2, design:** the two pipes and two capitals. They glow, then the pipes slowly bend towards each other and join.
- **Layer 3, choices:** four tags (blended price, no training line, moved readiness money, states' building gap), each with a small dial that turns.
- Final image: the patient's home at night, its window lit. The beam softens into warm light falling on the home.

**Narration:**
> So, who is to blame?
>
> Some of it is no one's fault. An older country and a slower economy squeeze everyone — insurers, hospitals, surgeons, governments — at the same time.
>
> Some of it is the design. When responsibility is split between two capitals, two pipes and a negotiating table, every problem can be passed along, and every actor can be right about someone else.
>
> And some of it is choices — which means it can change. A price that pays nothing for training or for the hardest cases. A readiness payment that moves money rather than adding it. Buildings the states pay for only in part. Rules that for years made the night in hospital the default.
>
> The direction — sending more patients safely home — is right. The pressure is real. But blame is the easy part. The harder question is ownership: who will be responsible for the whole journey, and be judged by what happens to the patient in that home?
>
> Until someone owns that, everyone will keep pointing.

**Cards (layer 3, the changeable choices):**
- *Price includes no training time*
- *Readiness money reallocated, not added*
- *States cover ~half of building needs*
- *Overnight-friendly rules for years*

**Final card:** *Did they come back? How much pain? The right operation? A surgeon to do it in ten years?*

## Production notes

- **Length:** about 1,150 words. If the voice runs long, trim chapter 1 first.
- **Positions in the cold open and chapter 6** are short summaries of published stances:
  - **Hospitals (DKG):** underfunding, especially of buildings.
  - **Insurers (GKV/AOK):** costs, volume and overcapacity.
  - **Surgeons (DHG):** training and materials.
  - **States:** planning powers.
  - **Berlin (BMG):** too many beds and too much "occasional care".

  Attribute them as "argues" and avoid quote marks around invented wording on screen.
- **Fairness check:** every side is shown as right about someone else and constrained by something above or beside it. The film blames the structure and named choices, never a single villain.
- **The Klinikum** appears only through published facts. Ask it for comment before publishing.
